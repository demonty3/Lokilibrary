What the check actually shows

A → B: the grid alone buys nothing, and it costs something. This is not a judgement call — it is provable from how the panels are built. A block element already encodes a sub-cell edge, so it expands onto the finer lattice exactly: ▀ becomes two full cells, ▙ becomes three. No information is invented and none is lost, so every shape in B is identical in outline to A. What is not identical is everything drawn as a glyph — the beings, the site labels, the foliage, the stars, the ☼. A glyph cannot be tiled into four cells, so it is drawn once and lands at half its screen size.

B → C: a finer rule changes texture, not shape. The ground re-grains and the horizon can land on a half-cell. The hills are the same hills, the cottages are the same cottages, the crust runs the same profile. Nothing about the drawing got better.

So the frozen kill clause is met on its measurable half: "the extra cells only make the marks finer while the shapes stay as crude as they are now." The shapes provably did not change. Whether the finer grain alone is worth the legibility cost is the half only your eye can settle.

The asymmetry is the real finding, and it is new. The scene is made of two kinds of matter. Block matter — terrain, silhouettes, buildings — tiles, holds its size, and can take a finer edge. Glyph matter — every being, every label, every star — cannot tile at all, and on a finer lattice it either shrinks or stays chunky. That is the mechanism behind the glance-value failure that ended the scale-1 question in August, now named rather than observed. Any future resolution move has to answer it.
